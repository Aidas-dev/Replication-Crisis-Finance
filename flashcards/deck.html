How to use a card

Read the goal line before the slide appears. Speak the say lines in your own words. Do not read them. Touch the numbers on the point-at line. End with the next line, then change the slide.

Title slide

Time: 15 seconds.

Goal: the room knows who speaks and what question the talk answers.

Say:

  • This is a review of a 2023 paper in the Journal of Finance.
  • The question is simple. Does finance have a replication crisis?
  • The authors answer no, and they build a new test and a new dataset to make the case.

Next: "Start with the charge they answer."

1 / 14 The claim under test

Time: 45 seconds.

Goal: the room knows the charge and the headline answer.

Say:

  • Several papers say that most published factors fail to replicate.
  • Two charges sit behind that claim. Results break when the method changes, and the rest come from searching too many signals.
  • Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen accept the question and report that over 80 percent of factors replicate.
  • The fight is not about arithmetic. It is about which test counts.

Point at: "over 80 percent".

Next: "Take the two charges one at a time."

2 / 14 The two challenges

Time: 55 seconds.

Goal: the two charges are different, and both sound serious.

Say:

  • Internal validity asks whether a result survives a change of method or data.
  • Hou, Xue and Zhang rebuilt 447 anomalies. 286 are insignificant at 5 percent, and 380 fail at a t-statistic above 3.
  • Their average alpha is 0.26 percent per month. They call the literature full of p-hacking.
  • External validity asks whether the result is real at all. Cochrane calls the field a factor zoo, and Harvey, Liu and Zhu raise the bar to a t-statistic of 3.
  • One charge says the results are fragile. The other says the search itself makes them false.

Point at: 286, 380, 0.26.

Next: "The paper answers with three moves."

If asked "What is p-hacking?": it means testing many signals and reporting only the significant ones.

3 / 14 Three moves

Time: 40 seconds.

Goal: the room sees the structure of the paper.

Say:

  • First, a model. It puts a prior on the true alpha and shrinks each estimate toward zero.
  • Second, a dataset. 153 factors in 93 countries, with public construction rules.
  • Third, a reassessment. The authors test the factors inside the sample, outside it, and in other countries.
  • The model comes first, because it decides what counts as evidence.

Next: "Start with the model."

4 / 14 The model

Time: 70 seconds.

Goal: alpha is the part of the return that the market cannot explain.

Say:

  • Each factor return splits into three parts. The market return, a constant alpha, and noise.
  • Alpha is the object of the test.
  • The prior says alpha is zero on average. A factor can deviate, but the model expects that most do not.
  • The estimate shrinks by kappa. Kappa is below one, and it moves toward one when the sample is long or the estimate is precise.
  • A short noisy backtest gets shrunk hard. A long precise one keeps most of its alpha.

Point at: the three equations.

Next: "That is why the paper prefers shrinkage to a p-value penalty."

If asked "Why not a normal t-test?": a t-test treats each factor alone. The model borrows evidence across all 153 factors.

5 / 14 Why shrinkage wins

Time: 60 seconds.

Goal: the Bayesian correction keeps power that a p-value correction loses.

Say:

  • Benjamini-Yekutieli raises every p-value. It controls false discoveries and loses power everywhere.
  • The Bayesian posterior learns how much to shrink from the same data.
  • It shrinks hard when true alphas are rare, and little when they are common.
  • In a simulation of 130 factors, the uncorrected test lets the false discovery rate reach 25 percent.
  • Both corrections fix that. Only the Bayesian one keeps the power to find real factors.

Point at: 25 percent.

Next: "Now the data."

If asked "What is a false discovery?": a factor reported as real that is truly zero.

6 / 14 The data

Time: 50 seconds.

Goal: the dataset is built, not scraped, and the rules are public.

Say:

  • The sample starts from 202 signals that Hou, Xue and Zhang catalogued. Those signals expand to 452 portfolios.
  • The authors keep one holding period and the latest accounting data, and add 15 factors. That gives 153.
  • Construction is deliberate. Tercile sorts, breakpoints from big stocks, capped value weights, and a 10 percent volatility scale for every factor.
  • The sample covers 93 markets. 23 are developed, 27 are emerging, and 43 appear only in the world portfolio.

Point at: 153, 93.

Next: "With the data in place, here is the headline."

7 / 14 The replication ladder

Time: 70 seconds.

Goal: the rate rises when the test matches the theory.

Say:

  • The grey bar is the original test on raw returns, at 35 percent.
  • The same test on this sample gives 55.6 percent. The sample is longer and the factors are built more carefully.
  • The big step is risk adjustment. CAPM alpha lifts the rate to 82.4 percent.
  • A multiple-testing correction costs some power at 75.6, and the Bayesian model returns to 82.4.
  • Read the ladder from bottom to top. The story is about the test, not the data.

Point at: 35.0, 55.6, 82.4, 75.6.

Next: "The result is not a micro-cap artifact."

If asked "Is 82 percent just a loose test?": the benchmark follows from theory, and card 12 shows what happens when you doubt the prior.

8 / 14 Size groups and themes

Time: 50 seconds.

Goal: the result does not live only in tiny stocks, and the factors group into themes.

Say:

  • Replication stays high across size. Mega caps reach 77.3 percent, small and micro caps 85.7, and nano caps 68.1.
  • So the result does not rest on tiny illiquid stocks.
  • The 153 factors fall into 13 themes. Eleven replicate in at least half of the specifications.
  • The two failures are low leverage and size. Ten themes earn a significant weight in the tangency portfolio.

Point at: 68.1.

Next: "Does it survive outside the United States?"

9 / 14 Out-of-sample decay

Time: 60 seconds.

Goal: returns fall after publication, and the model predicts the fall.

Say:

  • Global alpha is 0.49 percent per month in sample and 0.26 out of sample. That is a 47 percent fall.
  • McLean and Pontiff report a 58 percent fall, so the size is familiar.
  • The authors say this is not a failure. A Bayesian expects attenuation.
  • The slope of out-of-sample on in-sample alpha is 0.57 before publication, 0.26 after, and 0.35 pooled. The benchmark is 0.9.
  • Some results at the top were mined, but the average factor still earns a positive alpha.

Point at: 0.49, 0.26, 0.57.

Next: "Does it pay?"

If asked "What does attenuation mean?": the measured alpha falls toward its prior, because part of the estimate was noise.

10 / 14 Economic value

Time: 50 seconds.

Goal: the surviving factors carry real economic weight.

Say:

  • The market Sharpe ratio is 0.13 per month. The tangency portfolio of themes reaches 0.80.
  • That number uses in-sample weights, so the honest comparison is the cross-validated 0.56.
  • Factors that Bayes keeps and Benjamini-Yekutieli rejects earn 0.35 percent per month in the US, at a t-statistic of 5.1.
  • An information ratio near 1 says the gain is not just extra risk.

Point at: 0.13, 0.80, 0.56.

Next: "The main objection is about the prior."

If asked "Is 0.56 a high Sharpe ratio?": it is very high for a monthly figure, and it comes from a portfolio of published factors.

11 / 14 The main objection

Time: 60 seconds.

Goal: the answer depends on a prior that the data cannot fix.

Say:

  • Campbell Harvey accepts the arithmetic and disputes the prior.
  • Empirical Bayes estimates how many factors are true from the same data.
  • If few are true, the method is too optimistic, because it shrinks too little.
  • He holds the target false discovery rate at 5 percent and varies the share of true factors.
  • At a 50 percent share the Bayesian rate is 5.7 percent. At a 1 percent share it climbs to 56.4, while Benjamini-Hochberg stays near target.
  • The answer depends on a number that the data cannot pin down.

Point at: 56.4 and 4.7.

Next: "Two more objections matter."

If asked "Does that kill the paper?": no, it limits the claim. The paper is strongest when many factors are real.

12 / 14 Three more objections

Time: 55 seconds.

Goal: the paper has three further soft spots, and each is bounded.

Say:

  • Benchmark choice. Raw returns give 35 percent, and CAPM alpha gives 82.
  • The defense is that theory prescribes risk adjustment, but the looser test also gives the headline.
  • Decay. Returns fall after publication, and the authors answer that the model predicts the fall.
  • A 2025 test asks a different question. Can a standard six-factor model explain the factors?
  • It explains 132 of 153, so 21 remain.

Next: "So what is the takeaway?"

13 / 14 Verdict

Time: 50 seconds.

Goal: the narrow claim falls, the broad claim stays open.

Say:

  • The narrow claim is hard to defend now. Most factors survive when the test is a pricing model and the correction is Bayesian.
  • The broad claim stays open. How much of the factor zoo is real depends on the prior.
  • The data give a weak signal about that prior.
  • The dataset is the durable contribution. It is public, global, and it will outlast this debate.

Next: "That is the paper. Questions welcome."

Close

Time: 20 seconds.

Goal: end cleanly and invite questions.

Say:

  • Thank the room.
  • Repeat the citation and the data link.
  • Invite questions on the prior, the benchmark, or the data.

Point at: jkpfactors.com.

Likely questions

Why CAPM and not Fama-French?

Fama-French contains size and value. Those are factors under test, so the benchmark absorbs part of the answer.

How can this paper find 82 percent when other papers find 35?

The other papers test raw returns. This paper tests CAPM alpha. Risk adjustment moves the rate by about 27 points.

Does shrinkage throw away real factors?

It shrinks by precision. A precise estimate keeps most of its alpha. The simulation shows that the power loss is small when real factors are common.

Why do returns fall after publication?

Part of the in-sample alpha comes from search. Investors also trade away part of the rest. A Bayesian expects the estimate to fall toward the prior.

What is the false discovery rate?

It is the expected share of reported discoveries that are false. At 5 percent, one in twenty reported factors is false on average.

Where are the data and code?

The data are at jkpfactors.com. The code is at github.com/bkelly-lab/jkp-data. The code runs through WRDS, so the raw CRSP and Compustat data stay licensed.

What is the 2025 test?

A working paper asks whether a standard six-factor model can explain each factor. It explains 132 of the 153 factors, so 21 remain.

Is the result sensitive to the prior?

Yes. Card 12 shows the table. At a 1 percent share of true factors, the Bayesian false discovery rate is 56.4 percent.
